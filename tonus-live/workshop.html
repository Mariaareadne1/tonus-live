First Effects

étude is a live-coding language that reads like the part on your music stand. You write notes, bar lines, dynamics and bowings, and it plays them back in a loop while you edit. In this lesson you'll learn the effects: how to make a phrase dark or bright, loud or soft, long or short, close or far away.

Press ▶ on any example, or click into the code and press ctrl+enter. Edit while it plays and press ctrl+enter again to hear the change. ctrl+. stops everything.

Reading a score

If you can read a violin part, you can almost read étude already. Here are the open strings, then a held E:

writemeans
A4 C#5 Bb3A note: letter, then # or b, then octave. Middle C is C4, the violin's A string is A4. If you leave off the octave, you get octave 4.
| … |A bar. All the notes in a bar share it equally, so four notes in 4/4 are quarter notes and eight are eighths.
-A tie: hold the note before for one more slot, even across a bar line.
rA rest.
(E5 F5)A beamed group. The notes share one slot, so two make eighths and three make a triplet.
D4+A4A double stop: notes joined with + sound together.
A4*4The same note four times.
|: … :| x3Repeat signs. The passage plays twice, or as many times as x3 says.
%Simile: play the previous bar again.
tempo Allegro time 3/4 key D majorTempo (a marking or BPM), time signature and key signature, on their own lines. With a key signature, an F in D major plays F♯. Write Fn for a natural.
violin: cello: piano: …Starts a part. Several parts play together, like a score. Instruments: violin, viola, cello, bass, piano, harp, flute, organ, drums.

Everything at once, a little minuet with a repeat:

  • In D major, the F5 and C5 sound as F♯ and C♯. Change the key to key D minor and listen to the mood change.
  • The cello's % repeats its first bar.

We have notes and bars, so now let's look at effects. Effects go on their own lines under a part, and they change only that part.

Some basic effects

tone

tone is where your bow meets the string. Under the hood it's a low-pass filter: it takes away the high overtones.

  • Change tone 800 to tone tasto or tone 200. Notice how it gets muffled, like bowing over the fingerboard.
  • Now try tone ponticello or tone 5000. Right by the bridge it turns glassy and bright.
  • The named tones, from dark to bright: flautando tasto dolce normale ponticello.

pattern the tone

  • Try adding more values.
  • Notice that the pattern in tone doesn't change the rhythm. Each note just takes the next value in the list, then the list starts over.
  • Now write it with bar lines: tone | tasto | ponticello |. With bar lines you get one value per measure instead of one per note.

Later we'll learn how to change these smoothly with hairpins…

vibrato

  • The + stacks notes into triple stops. Each chord gets the next kind of vibrato.
  • The choices are none slow normal fast wide. A number sets the width in cents. Try vibrato 40.
  • Like a real player, the vibrato blooms a moment after the bow starts.

dynamics

Rhythm is all about dynamics!

  • Delete the dynamics line and notice how flat it sounds.
  • Bring it back by undoing (ctrl+z).
  • Use any of ppp pp p mp mf f ff fff, or a number from 0 to 1. Parts are mf unless you say otherwise.
  • drums hats: and drums kit: are two drum parts. The word after the instrument is just a label.

shape the sound with the bow

Try to find out what the numbers do. Compare these:

  • attack: 0.5 vs 0
  • decay: 0.5 vs 0
  • sustain: 1 vs 0.25 vs 0
  • release: 0 vs 0.5 vs 1

  • attack: how many seconds the bow takes to bite, from silence to full sound.
  • decay: how quickly the sound relaxes after that first bite.
  • sustain: how loud the note stays while the bow keeps moving, from 0 to 1. At 0 the note dies away like a pluck.
  • release: how long the string keeps ringing after the bow lifts at the end of the note.

You only need to write the ones you want to change.

bow strokes

You rarely need the numbers, because the strokes you already know are built in:

  • Change legato to detache, martele, staccato, spiccato and finally pizz.
  • Strokes can be patterned like everything else. Try bowing martele spiccato spiccato spiccato.
  • String parts play detache unless you say otherwise.

echo

  • Try some echo amounts between 0 and 1.
  • What happens if you write echo 0.8 sixteenth? Can you guess what the second word does?
  • What happens with echo 0.8 sixteenth 0.8? Can you guess what the third number does?
Click to see solution
  • The first number is how loud the echo is.
  • The second word is the time between echoes, as a note value: whole half quarter dotted-eighth eighth triplet sixteenth. The default is eighth.
  • The third number is how long it keeps bouncing back, from 0 (once) to 0.95 (almost forever).

hall, aka reverb

  • Try different halls: dry room studio chapel concert cathedral, or a number of seconds like hall 10.
  • Add an echo too!
  • Did you notice the B4 sounds as B♭? That's the key D minor signature at work.

little tune

Let's add a bass to make this complete:

  • Try writing tacet under one of the parts. Like in a real part, it means "don't play".

seat

  • Headphones help here. seat places the sound from left to right, like seats on a stage: left center-left center center-right right, or a number from -1 to 1.
  • In an orchestra the first violins sit on the conductor's left and the cellos on the right. Try a violin part with seat left and a cello part with seat right.

tempo & speed

tempo changes the whole piece. speed changes one part: half time, double time, or any number.

  • Change half to double. Then try speed 1.5.
  • Notice that the violin keeps its own pace. Only the drums changed.
  • Now change Moderato to Adagio or Presto. The markings are grave, largo, lento, adagio, andante, moderato, allegretto, allegro, vivace and presto, or just write a number in BPM.

hairpins

So far every value has changed in steps. With hairpins, < and >, values move smoothly instead:

  • The score roll under the code shows dynamics as brightness. Watch it swell.
  • Try ff > pp for a diminuendo, and pp < ff > pp for a messa di voce.
  • The hairpin stretches across the whole part, then starts again.

hairpins on tone and seat

Hairpins work on tone and seat too:

  • What happens if you flip it to 4000 > 500?
  • Try seat left < right on a new line.

over

Use over to stretch a hairpin across more bars:

  • The whole sweep now takes 8 bars to come back around. Try over 2 and over 16.

Composing with code

So far every note has been written out by hand. Composers rarely do that: they name a theme, repeat it, move it up a step, and change the ending the last time through. étude can do all of that, so this is where it starts to feel like programming.

let: name a phrase

  • let opening = … gives the phrase a name. Put the name between bar lines and the whole phrase plays there.
  • Watch the highlighting: the notes light up where the phrase is defined.
  • A phrase can carry on over several lines, as long as each new line starts with a bar line.
  • Names are words like opening or bridge. They can't look like a note (a, b4) or reuse a word étude already knows.

let: name a value

  • Variables also hold a single note, a little motif, or a value like a dynamic.
  • Change let root = D3 to G2, and every place that says root follows.
  • Try let loud = fff.

x2, up, down: multiply and transpose

  • x2 (or * 2) plays a phrase twice.
  • up 5 and down 12 transpose by semitones: 7 is a fifth, 12 is an octave.
  • You can combine them: riff up 5 x2.

numbers you can calculate

  • Anywhere étude wants a number, you can write a sum: + - * / and parentheses.
  • Change tempo pulse * 2 to tempo pulse * 1.5.
  • beat * 3 is 0.75 beats, a dotted-eighth echo.

repeat: loops

  • repeat 4: writes the bars after the colon four times, without copy-pasting.
  • It's like a repeat sign, but it can hold anything, including the next two ideas.

repeat … as n: a counter

  • as n names a counter that goes 1, 2, 3 … each time round.
  • step up n - 1 moves the pattern up one semitone each pass: a sequence, written once.
  • Try step up (n - 1) * 2 for whole steps, or step down n * 5.

if … then … else: conditions

  • The first three times, the theme ends up on E (a half cadence). The last time it comes home to D.
  • Conditions you can write: n is last, n is first, n is even, n is odd, n = 2, n > 2, n <= 3, n is not 1.
  • The else part is optional. Without it, nothing is added that time round.

first and second endings

You've been using conditionals for years: a repeat with first and second endings is an "if" written in music.

  • |1. starts the first ending, and :|2. jumps back and then takes the second ending.
  • It plays: bars 1–2, first ending, bars 1–2 again, second ending.
